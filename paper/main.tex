\begin{abstract}
Recordings of dogs playing with people contain the people's speech, which must be removed before the
recordings can be shared, while the dog vocalizations are the data. We study anonymization on 125
minutes of annotated dog--human social play recorded at the ELTE BARKS Lab (30 sessions, 17 dogs, 581
human sound events, 2{,}055 dog vocalizations). Privacy is measured by speech recognizers from two model
families acting as attackers (Whisper small and large-v3-turbo, NVIDIA Parakeet), and the cost by the dog
vocalizations lost, overall and per call type; every model is evaluated on dogs it never saw. Three
results. (1) A frame classifier on the frozen Whisper encoder and AudioSet class scores, trained to
separate human sounds from dog vocalizations, finds 84\% of the human sound frames at a cost of 6\% of
the dog frames (82\% with the call-type weights below), against 72\% for an AudioSet-only detector; muting its detections leaves 1.0\% of the
human events readable (unprocessed: 19.8\%; background noise alone: 1.0\%) and loses a third less dog
sound (95\% CI 21--51\%). (2) Weighting every dog call type equally in training keeps all 48 barks and
92\% of the grunts intact (previously 75\% and 66\%) at the same privacy. (3) Dog sounds under speech,
which muting always loses, can be partly kept: a dog extractor conditioned on Whisper's encoder, used only
where a router hears a dog call, returns a fifth of the muted dog sound with no measurable change in
privacy for any of the three attackers, and a third with a small privacy cost; the share of dog sound lost
falls from 16.4\% to 9.8\% (8.2\%). Adding steady noise with the recording's own room tone, 10\,dB above it,
where the router hears no dog lets the extractor's output fill \emph{all} the muted time: no dog sound is
silenced, and three attackers, including one that first removes the noise, read back no new words (only
laughter and backchannels); quiet calls come back at about the level of the added noise. We release the
code and a one-command anonymizer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Bioacoustic studies of dogs increasingly rely on recordings made during natural interaction with
people. In dog--human social play the human partner talks, laughs and calls the dog throughout, so a
recording that captures the dog's play pants, growls and barks also captures personal data: what was
said, and a voice that identifies the speaker. Before such a dataset can be shared, the speech has to
be removed. The dog sounds, however, are the point of the recording, and every second muted by mistake
is data lost.

Removing speech from environmental recordings has so far been treated as a voice activity detection
problem \cite{cretois2022,priebe2024}: detect speech, then delete or mute it. Speaker anonymization in
the sense of the VoicePrivacy challenge \cite{tomashenko2020voiceprivacy} is a different task: it keeps
the words and hides the identity. Here the words themselves must go. Two features of dog--human play
make detection hard. First, the dog vocalizations are acoustically close to human non-speech sounds:
a play pant is a breathy, forced exhalation \cite{simonet2005laughter}, not unlike a human laugh or sigh.
Second, the relevant cost is not generic false alarms but dog sound lost, so the detector should be
judged at a fixed loss of dog vocalizations, not at a fixed false-positive rate on all audio.

This paper makes five contributions. (i) An evaluation protocol for this setting: leave-one-dog-out
cross-validation, privacy measured by speech recognizers from two model families acting as attackers,
and the cost measured as dog vocalization time and dog call events lost, per call type
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}). (ii) A detector that combines a frozen speech encoder (Whisper
\cite{radford2023whisper}) with AudioSet class scores \cite{schmid2023efficientat} and is trained on
the trade-off that matters, human sounds against dog vocalizations (Section~\ref{sec:method}). It mutes
about as much speech as an AudioSet-only detector with a third less dog sound lost
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). (iii) Call-type-balanced training, which protects the rare calls (barks,
whines, moans) without any privacy cost. (iv) A way to keep part of the dog sound that overlaps speech:
a Whisper-conditioned dog extractor, applied only where a dog router hears a dog call
(Section~\ref{sec:keep}). (v) Negative results that narrow the design space: subtracting a speech
estimate, fusing three microphones for detection, deciding every 20\,ms, and dog extractors trained from
the spectrogram alone.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
The recordings were made at the ELTE BARKS Lab in Budapest between March and May 2025, during
dog--human social play in a laboratory room: 30 sessions of 17 dogs with their human play partners,
124.7 minutes in total, recorded at 48\,kHz with a shotgun (27 sessions) or cardioid microphone.
Up to two further microphones recorded in parallel; one of them, a wireless clip-on, is on the dog
(pants are about 8\,dB relatively louder on it than on the room microphone, human sounds about 4.5\,dB, in
25 of 30 sessions). Expert annotators
marked 581 human sound events (788\,s; speech, laughter, claps and other human sounds) and 2{,}055 dog
vocalizations of ten types. Panting dominates: 1{,}294 pants make up 3{,}521\,s of the 3{,}966\,s of
annotated dog sound (89\%). The other types are rarer: 198 growls, 163 grunts, 48 barks or yelps,
33 moans, 25 sneezes, 13 whines, 10 coughs or woofs, 7 shakes and 264 others.\todo{check with the lab: dataset name and citation, annotation protocol, consent and permission to publish}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Frames and features.} The audio is resampled to 16\,kHz and cut into 0.96\,s windows every
0.24\,s. Two frozen networks describe each window:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \emph{EfficientAT mn40} \cite{schmid2023efficientat}, a MobileNet distilled from transformer
ensembles on AudioSet \cite{gemmeke2017audioset}. Its convolutional feature map is computed on 10\,s
chunks (the model saturates on 1\,s inputs), averaged over the columns inside the window and passed
through its classification head: 527 class scores.
\item \emph{Whisper small encoder} \cite{radford2023whisper} (int8 ONNX export \cite{sherpaonnx}).
The audio is encoded in 30\,s windows every 20\,s, each 20\,ms frame taken from the window in which it
is most central (at least 5\,s of context on both sides). The 768-dimensional frames inside the 0.96\,s
window are averaged. The export provides the first decoder layer's cross-attention keys, a fixed
full-rank linear map of the encoder output, which carries the same information for a linear classifier.
Whisper's representations are known to retain background-sound information \cite{gong2023whisperat};
here the question is whether they separate human sounds from dog sounds better than AudioSet scores.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Classifier and objective.} A logistic regression ($C=0.05$, balanced class weights, standardized
inputs) is trained on frames that are at least half covered by a human event (positive) against frames
with no human sound that are at least half covered by a dog vocalization (negative). Frames with neither
are left out of training: muting background costs nothing the lab analyses, so the classifier should not
spend capacity on it. The output is smoothed over 5 frames ($\pm$0.48\,s).

\textbf{Call-type weights.} Panting is 89\% of the dog time, and only three dogs bark (21, 18 and 7
barks). A model trained without one of them has seen barks of only two other dogs and treats an unfamiliar
bark as ``not a pant'', i.e.\ possibly a voice. Each dog-only training frame is therefore weighted by
$(N/N_t)^{\alpha}$, where $N$ is the number of dog-only frames and $N_t$ that of its call type, and the
dog-only frames keep their total weight. With $\alpha=1$ every call type weighs the same.

\textbf{Thresholds and muting.} Thresholds are set where a fixed share $q$ of the dog-only frames is
flagged (3, 6, 10 or 15\%; the levels \emph{light}, \emph{standard} and \emph{strict} use 3, 6 and
10\%). Flagged frames are padded by 0.12\,s and merged, and these intervals are set to zero in the
original file. Everything else stays bit-identical.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\textbf{Protocol.} All results are leave-one-dog-out: each of the 17 dogs (with its human partner) is
processed by a model trained on the other 16. Thresholds are set on the pooled out-of-fold predictions; for the AudioSet-only
detector, choosing them inside the cross-validation instead gave the same results.

\textbf{Privacy.} A speech recognizer plays the attacker on each human event window (the event
$\pm$0.3\,s, loudness-normalized), before and after anonymization. We report the share of events where
it reads two or more words, and the share of the words it read in the original that it can still read
after muting (\emph{words read back}). Sound tags such as ``(dog barking)'' are not counted. Three
attackers are used: Whisper small (244M parameters) \cite{radford2023whisper}, Whisper large-v3-turbo
(809M), and NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 \cite{nvidia2025parakeet}, a FastConformer transducer trained
on different data that covers 25 European languages including Hungarian. Parakeet matters because the
detector itself uses Whisper's encoder. As a floor, the attackers also read background windows of the
same length with no human sound: Whisper small writes 2+ words in 1.0\% of them, Parakeet in 4.9\% and
turbo in 24.5\%, so for turbo only \emph{words read back} is meaningful. On background audio, the
share of an event's original words that turn up is 0.0\% for Whisper small and Parakeet and 1.6\% for turbo.

\textbf{Dog cost.} The share of dog-only vocalization time (dog annotated, no human annotated) that is
muted, and per call type the share of dog calls that remain \emph{intact} (not a single sample muted).
Calls that overlap a human sound are muted by design and reported separately.

\textbf{Dog calls by type.} Because 89\% of the dog time is panting, the time-based cost is mostly a
panting number. Table~\ref{tab:calls} counts calls instead. The new detector leaves more calls intact
for most types: 82\% of the play pants (previously 71\%), 84\% of the grunts (66\%). Barks are the main
exception (67\% vs 75\%, 48 events): the speech encoder appears to respond to the harmonic, voice-like
bark; sneezes also lose (83\% vs 92\%, 24 events). All 16 barks it muted came from one dog.
Call-type weights (last column) fix this: every call type keeps as many or more calls than with both
earlier detectors, except one sneeze, and privacy does not change (Whisper small: 1.0\% readable, 5.2\%
of the words; Parakeet reads back 7.9\% of its words instead of 9.2\%). This model is the default.

\textbf{Whole recordings.} The privacy numbers cover the annotated human events. To check for speech
the annotators did not mark, Whisper read all 8\,s windows with no annotated human sound within 0.5\,s
(519 windows, 68 minutes). Ten windows produced two or more words in the unprocessed audio: eight were
typical hallucinations over dog sounds and two were short phrases worth a listen. No substantial
unannotated speech was found.

\textbf{Out of domain.} The detector was also run, unchanged and with the thresholds set on ELTE, on
40 synthetic 30\,s scenes built from material it never saw: read English speech from 20 LibriSpeech
speakers \cite{panayotov2015librispeech}, 20 ESC-50 dog clips \cite{piczak2015esc}, rooms simulated with
pyroomacoustics \cite{scheibler2018pyroomacoustics} (RT60 0.2--0.8\,s) and pink noise, with three
utterances and four dog clips per scene that partly overlap. Whisper small, scored against the reference
transcripts, reads two or more correct words in 99\% of the unprocessed utterances. After the AudioSet-only
detector it still does in 11.7\% (light) and 7.5\% (standard); after the new detector in 1.7\% and 0\%,
as after muting the exact utterances (0\%). Silero VAD \cite{silero} leaves 11.7\%. The price is dog
sound: the new standard setting mutes 57\% of the dog-only time in these scenes, against 47\% for the
AudioSet detector and 47\% for the exact utterances, because foreign barks overlap the speech and are
hard to tell from it. An earlier attempt to reach this privacy by training the AudioSet detector on
synthetic speech-under-bark scenes needed 63--77\% dog loss.

\section{Keeping the dog under speech}
\label{sec:keep}
227\,s of dog sound lies under a human sound (223 calls, 178 of them pants); the standard setting mutes
184\,s of it. With the detector's false alarms it mutes 12.1\% of all dog sound, about 40\% of it under a
person.

\textbf{Dog extractor.} A network predicts a time--frequency mask $M\in[0,1]$ on the 16\,kHz
spectrogram (512-point STFT, 16\,ms hop) that keeps the dog: three convolution layers on the log power,
concatenated per frame with the Whisper encoder frames of the same audio (projected to 128 values), a
two-layer bidirectional GRU and a sigmoid output (1.2M parameters). It is trained to regress a
privacy-weighted Wiener mask, $|D|^2/(|D|^2+10\,|S|^2)$, with $D$ the dog and background and $S$ the human
sound, on mixtures of the lab's own audio: 4\,s windows with dog calls and no person, plus real
human-only events from the same participant group (30\% LibriSpeech speech in simulated lab rooms),
3{,}000 per group. Two folds split the participants (odd/even numbers); each session is processed by the
model of the other fold. On held-out mixtures it removes 6--12\,dB of the human sound while turning the
dog down by only 0.6--3.7\,dB (dog SI-SDR 9--15\,dB); the folds differ, with the remaining speech
20--22\,dB below the dog in one and only 2--4\,dB in the other. The same network without the Whisper frames removed
only 4--6\,dB: with $\sim$150 real human events per fold it learns the training voices, not speech.

\textbf{Where to use it.} Inside the muted time of the real sessions, replacing silence by the extractor's
output everywhere brings speech back (Whisper small: 2.8\% of the events readable instead of 0.9\%;
Parakeet reads back 16.9\% of its words instead of 7.9\%). Using it only inside the annotated dog calls
(an oracle) keeps privacy close to muting (1.2\% and 12.5\%): where the dog vocalizes, it covers what is
left of the speech. A \emph{router} therefore decides where a dog is calling: the human detector's
features, trained on frames with vs without a dog call (frames with a person weighted 5$\times$),
leave-one-dog-out. Inside human frames it separates dog+person from person-only with AUC 0.78 (0.81 with
the clip-on microphone cues). Its threshold is the share of person-only frames it lets through.

\textbf{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:keep}: with 2\% of person-only frames let through, Whisper small reads
exactly the same events and words as after muting, Parakeet reads back the same share of words (4 events
become readable, 6 stop being readable), and turbo reads back 17.9\% instead of 17.0\% of its words (the
gained words are fillers such as ``you'', ``so''); a fifth of the muted dog sound comes back and the share
of all dog sound lost falls from 12.1\% to 9.8\% (16.4\% for the AudioSet-only detector). At 5\%, a third
comes back (8.2\% lost); Whisper small reads the same words, Parakeet 10.4\% instead of 7.9\% (mostly
laughter), and turbo reads one short phrase in full that muting removes. With 5--54 readable events per
attacker, ``no measurable change'' is not a proof of equal privacy. The table's ``silence'' row comes from
its own transcription run and differs slightly from Table~\ref{tab:e2e}. With the clip-on microphone cues
the router lets more dog through at the same rate (38\% of the dog under a person at 5\%). The kept dog is
the extractor's output: 16\,kHz, a few dB quieter, with the speech removed (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). The
mask exponent 3 was chosen after exponent 1 had been scored on the same events (exponent 1, router 2\%:
Parakeet reads back 10.6\% of its words), so this setting is slightly optimistic; the router's threshold
is an average, and per participant the safe setting lets through 0--15\% of the person-only frames.

\textbf{Room tone.} Why does the extractor's output bring speech back when used everywhere? On clean
unseen voices it removes 14\,dB of the speech (20\,dB with $M^3$), but the attackers normalize the level
before reading, and in the quiet lab the leftover voice is still the loudest thing in the output: person-only
speech is about 15\,dB above the room tone, the extractor's output during it 4--6\,dB. We therefore add,
inside the processed time, stationary noise with the spectrum of the recording's own room tone (the
quietest 20\% of the frames outside the processed time), 10\,dB above it. With the extractor everywhere,
Whisper small reads back the same words as after muting and Parakeet 11.3\% (7.9\%); an adaptive attacker
that first enhances the audio with DeepFilterNet3 \cite{schroeter2023deepfilternet3} before Parakeet reads
15.6\% (10.0\% after muting). The extra events contain laughter, ``mm-hmm'' and ``thank you''---no new
words; turbo reads 17.6\% (17.0\%). Adding the noise only where the router hears no dog (``all'') keeps
the routed dog clean: Parakeet reads back 13.8\% ($+$5.8 points, 95\% CI 1.9--11.8 over participants, all
laughter, backchannels and ``thank you''), DeepFilterNet+Parakeet 16.1\%, turbo 18.4\% ($+$1.4, CI
$-$0.4--3.3; six events gain words, against four with the router alone). No dog time is muted; of the
432 annotated calls in the processed time, 34\% are 6\,dB or more above the added noise and 11\% get no
noise, but pants, the quietest calls (13\,dB above the room tone), come back at about the noise level
(median within 1\,dB). A noise level that follows the power the extractor removed kept the dog more audible
but let turbo read 5.5 points more, and was dropped.

\section{What did not help}
\label{sec:negative}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \emph{Subtracting instead of muting.} Where a dog overlaps the speech, subtracting a speech
estimate (DTLN \cite{westhausen2020dtln}, or GTCRN \cite{rong2024gtcrn} fine-tuned on this data) instead
of muting keeps some dog sound, but at equal dog loss it never left fewer readable words than muting.
\item \emph{Three microphones.} Pooling the training data of all microphones and taking the maximum
probability over them did not beat a single microphone with the same features.
\item \emph{20\,ms decisions.} About 40\% of the dog sound lost lies within 1\,s of a human sound, which
suggested finer boundaries. A classifier on every 20\,ms Whisper frame (with $\pm$0.06/0.5/1.5\,s
context, trained against all other frames) muted 68\% of the human time at 3.6\% dog loss, against 73\%
at 4.5\% for the 0.24\,s detector with the same input and objective (77\% at 7.1\% vs 81\% at 7.9\%);
interpolating the 0.24\,s probabilities to 20\,ms with tighter padding changed nothing.
\item \emph{Dog extractors from the spectrogram alone.} A privacy-weighted signal loss removed 19\,dB of
speech but also turned the dog down by 9\,dB; mask regression on the spectrogram (and with the clip-on
microphone's level difference) removed only 4--6\,dB of held-out speech.
\item \emph{Better extractors and routers.} 780 synthetic voices (Piper and VITS text-to-speech,
Hungarian and English, split by speaker between the folds), a loss on the leaked speech in dB, and a
U-Net-style frequency decoder each moved the extractor along the same speech-vs-dog trade-off (the
privacy-weighted target mask itself would remove 31\,dB). Describing the extractor's output with the
detector's networks did not make the router better (AUC 0.74--0.80).
\item \emph{Other inputs and labels.} Training on speech-only labels (events where Whisper reads words)
instead of all human sounds, normalizing the scores per recording, adding neighbouring frames or the
standard deviation of the Whisper frames were all equal or worse. Subtracting a separate dog detector's
score helped only with a tuned weight, and less than changing the training objective.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion and limitations}
The largest gains came from the input (a speech encoder) and from the training objective (human vs dog),
not from the classifier or the time resolution. The detector is a linear model on frozen features,
trained on 16 dogs in a few minutes on a laptop, and the full anonymizer, including both frozen networks,
runs at about 8$\times$ real time on two CPU cores.

The evaluation has limits. All real data come from one lab, one room and one kind of interaction; the
synthetic out-of-domain test covers other speakers, dogs and rooms only in simulation. Privacy is
measured by automatic recognizers on the annotated events; a human listening test, of the remaining
readable events and of the kept dog sound under speech, is still to be done (a pack of 12 clips is
prepared). The threshold fixes the dog cost on average: at the 6\% setting
the share of dog-only frames flagged ranges from 0\% to 19\% between dogs. Handler identity is not in the
metadata, so if one person played with several dogs, the split is disjoint in dogs but not in speakers.
The inputs, objective and settings were chosen over several rounds on the same 17 folds, which makes the
best configuration somewhat optimistic; the out-of-domain test is the guard against this. The kept dog sound under speech is a processed signal
at 16\,kHz, not the original recording, and the router finds only a third to a half of it at an
acceptable privacy cost; with room tone all of it comes back, but quiet calls under a noise floor. The extractor
passes laughter.

\section{Conclusion}
For anonymizing dog--human play recordings, a frozen speech encoder combined with AudioSet class
scores, trained on human versus dog sounds with every call type weighted equally, reduces the readable
speech to the level a recognizer reaches on background noise while losing a third less dog vocalization
than an AudioSet-only detector, and a Whisper-conditioned dog extractor, gated by a dog router, brings back
part of the dog sound under speech at no measurable privacy cost; with the recording's own room tone
masking what is left of the voice, no dog sound needs to be silenced at all. The code, the evaluation and a
one-command anonymizer that keeps the original file format are available at \todo{repository URL once
public}.
